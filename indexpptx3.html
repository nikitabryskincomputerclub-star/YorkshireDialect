The Yorkshire Dialect of English

A Linguistic and Cultural Overview

Prepared by: Bryskin Nikita Mikhailovich

4th-year Bachelor’s student, Group ZAR-231

Introduction

02 / 14
  • Yorkshire dialect is also known as Broad Yorkshire or Tyke.
  • Yorkshire is the largest county in England.
  • Its roots lie in Old English and Old Norse.
  • The dialect has a distinctive identity, celebrated on Yorkshire Day (1 August).

Historical Influences

03 / 14
  • Celtic substrate: river names (Calder, Nidd), brock (badger).
  • Anglo-Saxon settlement (5th c.): Angles in the north (Northumbria), Saxons in the south (Mercia).
  • Viking influence (9th c.): dale, sam, many place names.
  • Norman French: minor impact (urchin, buffit, fent).

The Great Dialect Divide

04 / 14
  • Two main zones: Mercian (south-west) vs Northumbrian (north-east).
  • Boundary roughly along the River Wharfe.
  • First noted by A. J. Ellis; confirmed by Joseph Wright.
  • Village-level mapping by Stodd (1906), Sheard (1945), Rohrer (1950).

Distribution and Sub-regions

05 / 14
  • Historical ridings: North, East, West (and South Yorkshire).
  • West Riding: harsher sound (industrial past); North/East: softer (rural).
  • Trudgill (1990): North/East in Northern group; South in Southern group.
  • Local distinctiveness: Hull/East Yorkshire accent; Middlesbrough similar to Geordie.
  • Sheard (1945) predicted standardisation of West Riding dialect; not fully realised.

Phonetic Features

06 / 14
  • Short /a/ in bath, dance.
  • Monophthongs in house → hoos, out → oot.
  • lang, wrang, strang; finnd, blinnd.
  • hee-am (home), stee-an (stone).
  • -in' for -ing; h-dropping; glottal stop.
  • East Yorkshire: partial rhoticity.
  • West Riding: devoicing (red tin → ret tin).
  • Regional variants: father (fatther vs feyther/faather), about (abaht vs aboot).

Lexical Features: Core Words

07 / 14
  • owt / nowt – something / nothing.
  • 'appen – perhaps.
  • 'eee by gum – exclamation.
  • middlin' – okay; nobbut middlin' – not great.
  • reet – very (reet bonny = very pretty).
  • summat – something (summat like that).
  • aye – yes.
  • sam – gather (cf. German sammeln).

Distinctive Vocabulary

08 / 14
  • bonny – pretty (a bonny lass).
  • lass – girl.
  • ginnel / snicket – alleyway.
  • gob – mouth (shut your gob!).
  • lug-ole – ear.
  • bray – to beat up.
  • roar – to cry (the baby is roaring).
  • gawping – looking; laiking – playing.
  • pots – dishes; any road – anyway.

Grammatical Features: Verbs and Nouns

09 / 14
  • were for all persons: It were dangerous; He were tall.
  • -s ending with I: I goes, I says.
  • No plural after numerals: two year old; twenty-four year.
  • them instead of those.
  • Definite article reduction: t' (ont bus).
  • nor instead of than (better nor her).
  • while instead of until.

Pronouns and Negation

10 / 14
  • me instead of my: that's me dad, that's me mum.
  • -sen instead of -self: mesen, thissen (shape thissen).
  • thee instead of you: How's thee?
  • tha / thar instead of your: How's thar do?
  • us for my / our: us house.
  • Multiple negation: I don't know naught about that; We didn't get no money.

Greetings and Everyday Expressions

11 / 14
  • Nah then? – How are you?
  • Ey up / Eh up – Hello.
  • Grand – really well.
  • Nicely – quite well.
  • Taken badly – ill.
  • Meals: breakfast; dinner or lunch; tea = evening meal.
  • To make tea – cook dinner vs brew tea; say a cup of tea to avoid confusion.
  • Christmas dinner – stays unchanged.

Modern Status and Preservation

13 / 14
  • Yorkshire Dialect Society founded 1897 by Joseph Wright.
  • Survey of English Dialects (Orton, 1950s–60s).
  • Publications: A White Rose Garland, Transactions, etc.
  • Dialect still used but changing under standard English influence.
  • Yorkshire Day celebrations keep heritage alive.

Conclusion

14 / 14
  • Unique blend of Celtic, Anglo-Saxon, Viking, and Norman influences.
  • Rich phonological, lexical, and grammatical heritage.
  • Lexical features prevail over grammatical ones; strong links to Old English survive.
  • Alive and evolving, a symbol of regional pride.
  • Deserves recognition and preservation.
